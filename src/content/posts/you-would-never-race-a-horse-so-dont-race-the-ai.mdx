Mentoring learners at Skill-Wanderer, I keep running into the same rather amusing moment. It comes up again and again, with different people, in almost the same words.

I will get to it in a minute. But first, let us look at something that most of us have never quite thought all the way through.

---

## We Would Lose Most Fair Fights

{/* IMAGE PLACEHOLDER: three mismatched contests side by side on a sunny grassy plain, like a very strange country fair. A runner in a vest watches a horse disappear over the horizon. A person with raised bare fists slowly backs away from a lion, which is yawning, completely unimpressed. A third person strains, red-faced, under a small log while an elephant beside them lifts a far bigger one with its trunk. No text. Gently humorous, painterly, wide 16:9. */}
<img src="/images/blog/you-would-never-race-a-horse-so-dont-race-the-ai/losing-fair-fights.jpg" alt="Three fair contests lost to a horse, a lion and an elephant" class="w-full h-auto rounded-lg shadow-lg mb-6" />

We can probably all agree that humans are the most advanced species on Earth.

But put us one-on-one against other animals, and it turns out we are not great at everything. To be honest, we are not great at most things.

Race an ordinary person against an ordinary horse, and it is very easy to see who is going to win. Fight a lion with your bare hands, and it is even easier. Try to out-lift an elephant, and the elephant will not even notice there was a contest.

Some people have actually tested the horse one. Since 1980, the small Welsh town of Llanwrtyd Wells has held a yearly Man versus Horse Marathon, which started, as these things often do, with an argument in a pub. It took until 2004 for a runner to win it, and runners have won only a handful of times since.

So if we lose most of the fair fights, why are we the ones called the most advanced, at least in the science books?

---

## So We Never Fight Fair

{/* IMAGE PLACEHOLDER: a construction site in bright daylight. High up in the cab of a giant yellow crane, a small human operator calmly lifts an enormous load with one lever. On the ground below, an elephant watches with its trunk raised, curious and a little impressed. Nearby, a horse inspects a parked pickup truck with deep suspicion. No text. Bright, warm, gently humorous, wide 16:9. */}
<img src="/images/blog/you-would-never-race-a-horse-so-dont-race-the-ai/the-crane-and-the-elephant.jpg" alt="A crane operator lifting a huge load while an elephant watches, impressed" class="w-full h-auto rounded-lg shadow-lg mb-6" />

Because we have a brain, and we use it to give ourselves an unfair advantage.

As a species, we do not compete cleanly. Or, to put it more kindly, we compete on different things.

We could not outrun the horse, so we built the car. A horse cannot drive a car. So if a human and a horse had to race today, the human would be driving, and nobody would bother betting on the horse.

We could not out-fight the lion, so we brought a gun. We could not out-lift the elephant, so we built cranes, trucks and a whole world of machines.

The winner was never the human with bare hands. **It was the human with tools, and now, the human with machines.**

## The Car Cannot Even Move on Its Own

{/* IMAGE PLACEHOLDER: a gleaming sports car sits alone on the starting line of an empty race track, the driver's door open, the seat empty, the keys resting on it. Far down the track, a horse trots past without a glance. Late afternoon light, long shadows. No text. Cinematic, quietly funny, wide 16:9. */}
<img src="/images/blog/you-would-never-race-a-horse-so-dont-race-the-ai/the-empty-car.jpg" alt="An empty sports car on the starting line, going nowhere without a driver" class="w-full h-auto rounded-lg shadow-lg mb-6" />

Now let us spice the competition up a little. What if the human has to compete against the tools themselves?

A human against a gun, for example. Who wins?

Obviously not the gun on its own. A gun lying on a table does not win anything. It is the gun with a person holding it. (I explored the same idea, with samurai and matchlock guns, in [the Tanegashima Shift](/blog/the-tanegashima-shift-the-tragedy-and-the-ascension-of-the-samurai-coder).)

The same goes for the car. The race is never a car against a human. It is a car with a driver against a human. A car by itself cannot beat anybody, because a car by itself cannot even move. Trucks and cranes are no different.

Yes, some cars can drive themselves now, in a growing number of cities. Even then, somebody still has to get in and tell the car where to go. Hold on to that thought.

Easy to see, right? And some of you may have already spotted the first lessons.

**Do not race a horse.** Or at least, never do it bare-handed. Bring a car, or at the very least a motorbike.

**Notice who actually wins.** Not the car. The human driving it.

Remember the marathon in Wales? It took until 2004 for a runner to beat the horses. But a human had already beaten them fifteen years earlier, back in 1989. His name was Tim Gould, and he was riding a bicycle.

Apparently, even a bicycle is enough.

## The Race My Learners Are Afraid of Losing

{/* IMAGE PLACEHOLDER: a stadium at night under bright floodlights. A young learner, laptop bag still over one shoulder, crouches nervously at the starting line of a running track, glancing sideways at the next lane, where a huge horse traced with softly glowing circuit-board lines paws the ground, ready to explode off the line. Empty stands. No text. Dramatic and slightly humorous, wide 16:9. */}
<img src="/images/blog/you-would-never-race-a-horse-so-dont-race-the-ai/racing-the-ai-horse.jpg" alt="A nervous learner on the starting line, next to a glowing AI horse" class="w-full h-auto rounded-lg shadow-lg mb-6" />

What if I told you this pattern is just as true in the AI era?

Here is the amusing thing I promised you. So many of the learners I mentor are worried that AI is going to replace them, because it can already do so much of their work better than they can.

The worry itself is not funny. It is real, and I take it seriously. What I find amusing is the race they are worried about losing.

If you race a horse, then yes, the horse is better than you. And if you race AI at the things AI is good at, then yes, AI is better than you too. **The worry is not wrong. The race is.**

It is easy to see how learners end up in that race. Universities still teach a lot of what AI is already good at: writing a loop, implementing a small, well-defined task. Train only for that, and you are training for a footrace against a horse. It is one of the reasons we rebuilt [the Skill-Wanderer curriculum around AI agents](/blog/wanderers-log-3-the-tet-reflection-why-skill-wanderer-is-going-ai-first).

## Do Not Race the Horse. Build the Car.

{/* IMAGE PLACEHOLDER: a cosy garage workshop at night. A learner with rolled-up sleeves works on a half-built car, surrounded by sketches, blueprints and tools. Through the open garage door, the horse with glowing circuit lines stands in the moonlit yard, waiting and unbothered. No readable text. Warm workshop light against a cool blue night, wide 16:9. */}
<img src="/images/blog/you-would-never-race-a-horse-so-dont-race-the-ai/building-the-car.jpg" alt="A learner building a car at night while the AI horse waits outside" class="w-full h-auto rounded-lg shadow-lg mb-6" />

So how do you beat a horse?

The first lesson is the one our ancestors learned: do not do it bare-handed, and focus on what the horse is not good at. Horses are fast and strong. They are terrible at building cars.

In AI terms: **do not try to beat AI at what it is already good at. Compete where it is weak, then bring that advantage home.**

Our ancestors did not win by training harder to run. They won by doing something the horse could not do at all, and then they came back to the same race with a car.

There is one more thing worth noticing. In this story, AI plays both parts. **The same AI is a horse if you race it, and a car if you drive it.** The AI does not change. What changes is where you put yourself: on the starting line next to it, or in the driver's seat.

So what is AI not good at?

---

## What AI Is Still Bad At: Ideas and Control

{/* IMAGE PLACEHOLDER: the view from the driver's seat on a winding mountain road at dusk. The driver's hands grip the wheel firmly, guiding the car through a sharp bend. On the passenger seat lies an unfolded paper map with one destination circled by hand. The dashboard glows faintly with lines of code. No readable text. Cinematic and focused, wide 16:9. */}
<img src="/images/blog/you-would-never-race-a-horse-so-dont-race-the-ai/ideas-and-control.jpg" alt="A driver's hands on the wheel, with a destination circled on the map beside them" class="w-full h-auto rounded-lg shadow-lg mb-6" />

Two things, mainly: **ideas** and **control**.

**Ideas.** AI still cannot set its own tasks. It does not wake up one morning knowing which app is worth building, who it is for, or why. Deciding where to go is still a human job. Remember the self-driving car: even when it does all the driving, somebody still has to tell it where to go.

**Control.** The quality of AI work has improved a lot, and I mean a lot. But left on its own, it is still pretty lousy. It makes confident mistakes, drifts away from what you asked for, and declares victory too early. Somebody has to steer it, check its work, and know when to hit the brakes. (I wrote about both sides of this in [the middle way on AI](/blog/the-middle-way-ai-is-neither-stupid-nor-a-silver-bullet).)

Put those two together and you have a fairly good description of a driver. The driver chooses the destination and keeps the car on the road. The car brings the speed. Everything else comes from the person behind the wheel.

## You Do Not Lose to the Car. You Lose to the Driver.

{/* IMAGE PLACEHOLDER: a tidy garage holding several very different vehicles side by side: a fast sports car, a sturdy pickup truck, a nimble motorbike and a delivery van, each traced with faint glowing circuit lines. In the foreground, a person holds up a ring of keys, deciding which one to take today. No text. Warm, cinematic, wide 16:9. */}
<img src="/images/blog/you-would-never-race-a-horse-so-dont-race-the-ai/the-garage.jpg" alt="A garage of different vehicles, and a person choosing which keys to take" class="w-full h-auto rounded-lg shadow-lg mb-6" />

Which brings me to the other half of the lesson, and the part I most want my learners to hear.

When a runner loses a race to a car, they are not losing to the car. They are losing to the person driving it.

We are not at the point where AI can run a piece of work from start to finish on its own. At least, not yet. So if you lose this race, you will not lose it to AI. **You will lose it to a human who uses AI**, and more and more often, to a human who uses several of them at once.

I personally use a lot of AI agents, because each one has its own strengths and weaknesses, and I match each one to the work it does best. It is [surprisingly similar to leading a team of people](/blog/leading-ai-agents-is-not-much-different-from-leading-people). It is also a bit like owning a garage: you do not take the truck to the race track, and you do not move house in a sports car.

So the competition was never you against the machine. **It is you against the people who have learned to drive it.**

---

## Learn to Drive, Not to Run

{/* IMAGE PLACEHOLDER: early morning in a city park. A jogger with earphones runs along a tree-lined path, smiling, clearly running for fun and fitness. On the road beside the park, a person drives calmly to work, a coffee in the cup holder. Both look content. Soft sunrise light. No text. Warm and optimistic, wide 16:9. */}
<img src="/images/blog/you-would-never-race-a-horse-so-dont-race-the-ai/running-for-health.jpg" alt="A jogger running for fun while a driver heads to work on the road beside the park" class="w-full h-auto rounded-lg shadow-lg mb-6" />

So if you want to beat the horse, the skill to learn is not running. It is driving. They are simply different skills, and getting faster at one does nothing for the other.

And yes, AI does make some old skills obsolete, or at least changes what they are for. Plenty of people still run today, but they run for their health, not to get to work. Nobody plans their commute around their 10K time.

The skills AI is already good at are heading the same way. Writing a loop by hand is still good exercise. It keeps you fit, and it helps you understand what the AI hands back to you. Just do not make it the race you are training to win.

**Focus on the skills that matter, not the ones AI is already good at.** Knowing where to go. Keeping things on the road. Picking the right vehicle for each trip. That is driving, and it can be learned.

If you read [my last post](/blog/i-was-not-born-for-this), you might be smiling by now, because I confessed there that I cannot drive a car. I chose not to, since a taxi gets me anywhere I need to go. That works fine for getting around town. It does not work in a race, because nobody has ever won one from the back seat. So with AI, I made the opposite choice.

So tell me: which race have you been training for? And is it one you can actually win?
