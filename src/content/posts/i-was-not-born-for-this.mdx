If there is one thing I want every learner who passes through Skill-Wanderer to walk away with, it is not a framework, a language, or a certificate.

It is **the ability to self-learn.**

Frameworks expire. Languages drift out of fashion. Certificates quietly go stale in a drawer. The ability to pick up the next thing on your own, without waiting for someone to package it into a course for you, is the one skill I know of that keeps paying out no matter what the industry decides to do next.

In [my last post](/blog/the-most-important-skill-in-tech-is-learning), I tried to pin down what learning actually is, after watching a whole argument collapse over a word nobody had bothered to define. Since then I have kept turning over self-learning in particular, and I realised something that comes before all of it. A step so early that most of us walk straight past it without noticing it is there:

**Before you decide to learn anything, you have to believe that you can learn it.**

That sounds almost too simple to be worth writing down. It is a lot harder than it sounds, and for good reasons. This post is about those reasons: where the belief comes from, why it is so convincing, what the research actually says about it, and how mine finally broke.

It is also about the other half, the half that motivational posters tend to leave out. Being able to learn something is not the same as having to learn it. Some of the things I am bad at, I am bad at on purpose.

## "I Was Not Born for This"

{/* IMAGE PLACEHOLDER: a young adult sitting at a desk, staring in defeat at a thick book wrapped in a chain and closed with a heavy padlock. The key hangs on a cord around their own neck, in plain sight but unnoticed. No text. Soft warm light, quiet and slightly melancholic, wide 16:9. */}
<img src="/images/blog/i-was-not-born-for-this/the-padlock.jpg" alt="A book locked shut, with the key hanging unnoticed around the reader's own neck" class="w-full h-auto rounded-lg shadow-lg mb-6" />

Across my career as a mentor and as a manager, there is one sentence I have heard more than any other, and every time I hear it, it sounds strangely nostalgic:

**"I was not born for this."**

It comes in plenty of costumes. *I am not a numbers person. I am just not technical. I am not a natural leader. I do not have the brain for code. Some people just get it, and I am not one of them.* Different words, same sentence underneath.

And here is the uncomfortable thing I have come to believe about it: **if you believe you were not born for something, you are 100% right.** As long as you believe it. And only for as long as you believe it.

That is not a trick of wording. Think about what the belief actually does. If you are convinced you were not born to code, you do not open the editor. If you open it anyway, the first error message reads like a verdict instead of a clue, and you close it again. If you were not born to lead, you turn down the team lead role, or you take it and spend every meeting waiting to be found out. Either way, you never collect the hundreds of small, clumsy, educational attempts that turn a beginner into someone competent.

So the belief does not describe your ability. **It manufactures the evidence that proves it right.**

The sociologist Robert K. Merton gave this pattern a name back in 1948: the [self-fulfilling prophecy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Self-fulfilling_prophecy), a belief that starts out false, changes how people behave, and in doing so makes itself come true. "I was not born for this" might be the most common self-fulfilling prophecy in all of learning. It is certainly the one I have heard most often from across a desk.

The only good news hidden in that sentence is its last part: *only for as long as you believe it.* The prophecy needs you to keep feeding it. The moment you stop, it stops being true.

That is easy for me to say now. It was not easy to live, because for a long time, I was the one saying it. Which is probably why it sounds so nostalgic.

## Two Things I Was Sure I Was Not Born For

{/* IMAGE PLACEHOLDER: a university student at a cluttered dorm desk at night, leaning back with arms crossed, deliberately pushing two objects to the far edge of the desk: a laptop whose screen is full of red error messages, and a team captain's armband. Through the window, a campus at dusk. No readable text. Nostalgic film-photo look, warm tungsten light, wide 16:9. */}
<img src="/images/blog/i-was-not-born-for-this/the-two-beliefs.jpg" alt="A student pushing away a laptop full of errors and a captain's armband" class="w-full h-auto rounded-lg shadow-lg mb-6" />

From childhood all the way through university, I carried a long list of things I believed I was simply not born for. Two of them matter for this story, mostly because they turned out to be the two things my working life would be built on.

**I believed I was not born to be a leader.** And let me be honest about this one: about the "born" part, I was right. I was not born a leader. That is not modesty. There is no doubt about it whatsoever.

**I believed I was not born for programming.** My reasoning felt airtight at the time. I am not a very careful person. I knew, with total certainty, that I would make bugs. And in my head, programmers were careful, precise people who wrote correct code, which made me very obviously not one of them.

The reasons behind both beliefs were surprisingly simple. **I believed talent was something you were born with.** You either had it or you did not, and the people who had it were visible from a mile away. Leaders led. Programmers programmed. Everyone else watched.

And the culture around me did an excellent job of confirming it.

## We Only Ever Meet Them at the Top

{/* IMAGE PLACEHOLDER: a dark cinema. On the screen, a heroic figure stands triumphant on a summit in golden light. Behind the audience, the projector's film reel has spilled across the floor, and the spilled frames show everything that was cut: someone practising alone at night, falling, studying, failing, repeating the same motion again and again. No text. Cinematic, high contrast, wide 16:9. */}
<img src="/images/blog/i-was-not-born-for-this/the-cut-footage.jpg" alt="The triumph on screen, and the years of practice left on the cutting room floor" class="w-full h-auto rounded-lg shadow-lg mb-6" />

Think about how we meet great people in films, in books, and in the stories we grow up on.

We meet the general on the morning of the decisive battle, already brilliant. We meet the founder on stage, already successful. We meet the leader in the middle of the famous speech, already magnetic. The years before, the awkward ones, the failed ones, the boring ones, either never make it into the story or get compressed into a two-minute training montage with good music.

From a storytelling point of view, that editing is perfectly reasonable. Nobody wants to watch ten years of someone slowly becoming slightly less bad at something. But it leaves us with a badly distorted picture. **We see great people when they are great. We almost never see what they were before, and we certainly do not see how they became great.** So we fill the gap with the simplest explanation available: they must have been born that way.

What surprised me is that this is not just a problem for teenagers watching films. Two Harvard researchers, Chia-Jung Tsay and Mahzarin Banaji, [ran a study with 103 professional and conservatory-trained musicians](https://banaji.sites.fas.harvard.edu/research/publications/articles/2011_Tsay_JESP.pdf). Asked directly, the musicians said that effort and training matter more than natural talent. Then they heard recordings of two pianists, one described as a "natural" and the other as a "striver", and were asked to judge them. They rated the natural as more talented, more likely to succeed, and more hireable.

Both recordings were of the same pianist.

These were experts, people who had personally put in thousands of hours of practice. And even they, when it came to an actual judgement, quietly preferred the story of the born genius over the story of the hard worker. The researchers called it the **naturalness bias**.

If trained professionals who know better fall for it, a kid watching films never stood a chance. I certainly did not.

## Most Great Leaders Were Not Born Great

{/* IMAGE PLACEHOLDER: a museum gallery wall with five grand framed portraits of historical leaders, faces softly lost in shadow so no one is identifiable. Beside each grand portrait hangs a small, plain pencil sketch of the same figure before: a nervous young lawyer in a courtroom, a frontier boy reading by firelight in a log cabin, a shop clerk behind a counter of leather goods, a teacher at a chalkboard, a peasant boy holding a pair of straw sandals. No text or plaques. Soft gallery lighting, quiet and reverent, wide 16:9. */}
<img src="/images/blog/i-was-not-born-for-this/before-the-portraits.jpg" alt="Grand portraits of leaders, each hung beside a humble sketch of who they were before" class="w-full h-auto rounded-lg shadow-lg mb-6" />

To be fair to my younger self, the "born for it" belief is not completely wrong. **Some people really are born with traits that give them a head start in leadership.**

Researchers have studied this using identical and fraternal twins, and the effect is real. A [2006 study of male twins by Richard Arvey and colleagues](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.leaqua.2005.10.009) estimated that about 30% of the differences between people in whether they end up holding leadership roles could be traced to genetics. A [later twin study](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S1048984312000811) put the figure at around a quarter.

So genes matter. But read those numbers from the other side. **Around 70% or more of the story is not explained by what you were born with.** It comes from what happens afterwards: experience, environment, opportunity, and learning.

And leadership turns out to be very learnable. Like most skills, it is built the unglamorous way: through practice, through feedback, and through getting things wrong and adjusting. That is worth remembering for later in this story.

History is full of people who were very obviously not born great.

### The lawyer who could not ask a single question

As a young barrister in Bombay, Mohandas Gandhi took his first case in the Small Causes Court. When the moment came to cross-examine the witnesses, he froze. In [his own words](https://www.mkgandhi.org/autobio/chap28.php):

> *"I stood up, but my heart sank into my boots. My head was reeling and I felt as though the whole court was doing likewise. I could think of no question to ask."*

He sat down, told the agent he could not conduct the case, and handed back his fee. That same man went on to lead India's independence movement. Whatever he became, he was clearly not born as it.

### The boy with less than a year of school

[Abraham Lincoln](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abraham_Lincoln) was born in a one-room log cabin on the Kentucky frontier. Writing about himself in the third person in 1860, he estimated that ["the aggregate of all his schooling did not amount to one year."](https://www.abrahamlincolnonline.org/lincoln/speeches/autobiog.htm) He taught himself most of what he knew, law included, from borrowed books. He lost his first election. He tried and failed to get into the US Senate twice. Then he became president, and he still sits near the top of almost every ranking of American presidents.

For a post about self-learning, it is hard to find a better example.

### The clerk in his father's leather shop

In 1860, [Ulysses S. Grant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ulysses_S._Grant) was 38 years old and working as a clerk in his father's leather goods store in Galena, Illinois. He had left the army six years earlier, struggled to scrape a living from a farm near St. Louis, sold firewood to get by, and watched one small business attempt after another fail. Less than four years later, he commanded every army of the Union. Later still, he served two terms as president.

Nobody looking at that clerk in 1860 would have picked him out as a born general.

### The history teacher who became a general

Closer to home, [I have written before](/blog/chatgpt-called-me-unconventional-it-unlocked-a-lesson-from-vietnams-greatest-general) about General Võ Nguyên Giáp. He was a history teacher, not a military academy graduate, and he never had formal military training. He taught himself strategy, and became one of the most formidable military minds of the twentieth century.

### The sandal-bearer who ruled Japan

And in [another post](/blog/the-tanegashima-shift-the-tragedy-and-the-ascension-of-the-samurai-coder) I told the story of Toyotomi Hideyoshi, born a peasant without even a surname, who started out carrying Oda Nobunaga's sandals and ended up ruling all of Japan.

None of these people were born great. Some of them surely had natural gifts. But in every one of these stories, the greatness arrived much later, after years of learning that the highlight reel leaves out.

As for me, I was certainly not born a leader. It turns out that puts me in rather good company.

## Nobody Was Born a Programmer

{/* IMAGE PLACEHOLDER: a single wooden desk holding three generations of programming side by side. On the left, a 1940s engineering logbook with a real moth taped onto the page beside illegible handwritten notes. In the middle, a 1980s continuous-feed code printout covered in red pen corrections. On the right, a modern laptop showing a failing test in red next to an AI chat panel. Warm desk-lamp light, documentary still-life style, wide 16:9. */}
<img src="/images/blog/i-was-not-born-for-this/every-era-has-bugs.jpg" alt="A 1947 logbook moth, a 1980s printout full of red ink, and a modern failing test" class="w-full h-auto rounded-lg shadow-lg mb-6" />

The programming belief was built on a different misunderstanding, and it took me an embarrassingly long time to see it.

I thought good programmers did not make bugs. **The one thing I did not know at that stage was that every developer makes bugs.** All of them, including the very best who have ever lived. Donald Knuth, about as close to programming royalty as anyone gets, once published a list of more than 850 errors he made while building a single program. And nowadays, even AI makes them, which makes sense when you remember who it learned to code from: us.

So the skill was never "do not make bugs". **The skill is catching them**: testing, reviewing, debugging, and building habits and systems that turn your carelessness into something manageable. Every one of those is learned. Nobody is born knowing how to read a stack trace.

Which brings me to the strongest argument I know against "born to code". **Nobody was born a programmer.** For the first generation it was literally impossible, because when they were born there was nothing to program yet. They had to work it out for themselves, and every programmer since has had to learn it too, one way or another. Me included.

To be fair, the picture has shifted a little. Today, with AI, someone who has never studied programming can get a simple app working, and that is a real change. But back when I started, there was no AI to lean on at all. Every line I wrote, I had to learn to write. And even now, with every tool available, building a complex application or a real platform is not something you can do without proper learning. You need to know what to ask for, how to tell when the answer is wrong, and how to hold the whole system in your head while the AI works on one piece of it. I have written about this at length in [the middle way on AI](/blog/the-middle-way-ai-is-neither-stupid-nor-a-silver-bullet) and in [leading AI agents](/blog/leading-ai-agents-is-not-much-different-from-leading-people). The tools changed *what* you have to learn. They did not remove the need to learn it.

## 100% Right, or 50% Right

{/* IMAGE PLACEHOLDER: an ancient Roman-era boat race on a bright Mediterranean sea. Two long oared ships near a rocky turning marker; the trailing crew rows with fierce determination, spray flying, closing the gap on the leading ship by inches. No text. Painterly, epic and sunlit, in the style of a classical oil painting, wide 16:9. */}
<img src="/images/blog/i-was-not-born-for-this/they-can-because-they-think-they-can.jpg" alt="A trailing crew in an ancient boat race, closing the gap on the leader" class="w-full h-auto rounded-lg shadow-lg mb-6" />

There is a saying I have carried around for years, and I have never managed to shake it:

> **If you go into a battle believing you are going to lose, you are 100% right. If you go in believing you are going to win, you are right maybe 50% of the time.**

What I like about it is that it is more honest than most motivational quotes. It does not promise that believing in yourself makes you win. It only points out the asymmetry. **Disbelief guarantees the loss. Belief merely keeps the outcome open.** And at least with 50%, you have a chance. With a guaranteed loss, you have none.

The idea is very old. About two thousand years ago, in the *Aeneid*, Virgil described a boat race in which a crew rowing up from behind is summed up in one line: "they can, because they think they can." What gets quoted far less often is how that race ends. The crew drew almost level with the leader, and still finished second. Virgil, it seems, understood the 50% as well.

Modern psychology broadly agrees. People who doubt they can do something tend to avoid it, put in less effort when they do try, and give up sooner when it gets hard. That is the 100%: believing you will lose does not predict the defeat, it arranges it. On the other side, research on the "growth mindset" (the belief that abilities can be developed) finds that believing you can improve does help, but modestly, and mostly when the environment supports it. Real, but not magic. That is the 50%.

**Believing you can learn something does not make you learn it.** It does not replace the practice, the good teacher, the right environment or the hours. What it does is make all of those possible. Belief does not win the battle. It only gets you onto the field.

Disbelief is the one option that ends the battle before it starts.

For most of my early life, I went into the battle believing I would lose. And I was 100% right.

## How the Belief Finally Broke

{/* IMAGE PLACEHOLDER: dusk at a busy container port. A lone figure in office clothes, holding a folder of shipping documents, stands at the edge of the quay. In front of them, a narrow footbridge made of softly glowing lines of code stretches out over the water toward a distant city of lit screens. The bridge is still being built, one plank appearing at a time just ahead of their feet. Cranes and stacked containers behind them. No readable text. Cinematic and hopeful, wide 16:9. */}
<img src="/images/blog/i-was-not-born-for-this/the-leap.jpg" alt="From a container port to a bridge of code, built one plank at a time" class="w-full h-auto rounded-lg shadow-lg mb-6" />

It only started to change when two things happened.

{/* TODO: confirm the book. The draft said "a Mark Anthony book", and I could not pin down which author or title was meant. Put the exact title here, and if one idea from it cracked the belief, quote it in this paragraph. */}

The first was a book by Mark Anthony. A book cannot make you a leader, but the right one can make you suspicious of the story you have been telling yourself, and that is what this one did.

The second was not something I chose at all. Through a chain of events that I still cannot decide was unfortunate or fortunate, I became an import and export manager. The job was about finding and reaching customers overseas, and it came with the one thing I had spent my whole life believing I was not built for: people to lead.

There was no opting out. The role needed a leader, and I was the one in it. Whether I felt born for it was beside the point.

The journey was never easy. But somewhere along the way, I noticed two things. The first was that I was improving. The second was that **I was not half as bad as I had thought.** Looking back, a good part of the distance between the leader I feared I was and the leader I actually was had been made of belief.

Notice what did the work there. Not inspiration, and not confidence. The book planted the doubt, but what actually changed me was having to practise, every day, with real stakes and real feedback. That is the same unglamorous way most leaders are made.

The programming belief went the same way, except this time I jumped instead of being pushed.

While I was still in import and export, our company set out to build its first website, and out of pure curiosity I took an HTML and CSS course on a free [LinkedIn Learning](/blog/the-skill-wanderers-playbook-a-deep-dive-review-of-linkedin-learning) trial. That was the spark. A friend, who became one of my early mentors, eventually convinced me to quit my import and export job and move into tech properly. It was a leap of faith, and it was not easy either. There were bugs, of course. Plenty of them, exactly as I had predicted all those years earlier. What I eventually learned was the thing I had not known back then: everybody makes them. The bugs were never proof that I was not born for this. They were simply part of the job.

Neither move proved that I had secretly been born for it all along. I had not been. What they proved was the thing I had been stubbornly refusing to believe: **you do not need to be born with it, because you are able to learn it.**

## Being Able to Learn Anything Is Not the Same as Learning Everything

{/* IMAGE PLACEHOLDER: a small market stall where skills are sold as objects: a stethoscope, a badminton racket, a car key, a laptop, a microphone, a paintbrush, a stack of course notes. Instead of prices, each item carries a paper tag with a small hourglass drawn on it, the sand at a different level on each. A customer's hand hovers over them, choosing. No text. Warm afternoon light, thoughtful mood, wide 16:9. */}
<img src="/images/blog/i-was-not-born-for-this/paying-in-time.jpg" alt="A market stall of skills, each priced in time rather than money" class="w-full h-auto rounded-lg shadow-lg mb-6" />

Here is where I want to be careful, because this is the point where posts like this usually tip over into motivational-poster territory.

"You can learn anything!" True, as far as it goes. But **being able to learn anything does not mean you can learn everything at once. And it certainly does not mean you should.**

Every skill has a price, and it is never only one kind of price.

There is **money**: courses, books, equipment, tuition, and the income you give up while you retrain.

There is **time**: the hours of practice, and the months of being bad at something before you are merely okay at it.

And there are **constraints that have nothing to do with ability at all.** I genuinely believe you could teach yourself medicine. The textbooks exist, the lectures are online, and the knowledge is learnable. But you would end up without a licence, and in most of the world practising medicine without one is illegal, for very good reasons. Some doors are not locked by talent. They are locked by credentials, by law, and by the years of supervised training a society reasonably demands before it lets you near a patient. The same goes for flying a commercial aircraft or signing off on a bridge. You can learn the material. That does not make you a doctor, a pilot, or a licensed engineer.

So I think about learning a bit like buying something. In principle, you can afford almost anything. You cannot afford everything at once, and every purchase rules out a few others. Like any purchase, it deserves a moment of thought before you hand over the money. **And the currency that matters most here is time, because time is the one you can never earn back.**

The writer Oliver Burkeman built a whole book, *Four Thousand Weeks*, around one sobering piece of arithmetic: if you live to eighty, you get roughly four thousand weeks. That is the budget. Every skill you decide to learn is paid for out of it, and every hour spent getting slightly better at one thing is an hour not spent on something else.

Being mindful of that is not pessimism. It is simply what it looks like to take your own time seriously.

## The Things I Choose to Be Bad At

{/* IMAGE PLACEHOLDER: a bright, tidy room. On a shelf, a badminton racket and a car key sit under a light layer of dust, clearly untouched for a long time. Below them, a desk in active use: a well-worn laptop, open notebooks, sticky notes. Through the window, a taxi waits outside. No text. Calm, content, gently humorous, wide 16:9. */}
<img src="/images/blog/i-was-not-born-for-this/chosen-to-be-bad.jpg" alt="A dusty badminton racket and car key, with a well-used desk and a taxi waiting outside" class="w-full h-auto rounded-lg shadow-lg mb-6" />

Which brings me to a list I am oddly proud of: the things I am bad at on purpose.

**I am a bad badminton player.** Not because I cannot learn badminton. I am quite sure I could, given the hours. I am bad at it because I chose to be. When I weigh what getting good would cost me in time against what it would give back, I see very little benefit in it for the life I actually live. So I leave it alone, and I am entirely at peace with being bad at it.

**I cannot drive a car.** Again, not because I am incapable of learning. I chose not to, because I can take a taxi anywhere I need or want to go, and I do not travel by car very much anyway. Learning to drive, and keeping the skill sharp, would cost real time for very little return.

And that list is nowhere near complete. There is plenty more I am bad at. But almost every item on it got there **by decision**, not by destiny.

From the outside, "cannot" and "chose not to" look identical. Either way, I will not be beating you at badminton or driving you to the airport. From the inside, they could not be more different. One is a belief about my limits. The other is a decision about my priorities. One closes a door I never actually tried. The other walks past a door I know I could open, because I would rather spend the time behind a different one.

Business strategy has a name for this. In their book *Uncommon Service*, Frances Frei and Anne Morriss argue that organisations that try to be good at everything end up mediocre at everything, and that excellence requires you to ["dare to be bad"](https://www.forbes.com/sites/hbsworkingknowledge/2015/06/17/to-acheive-excellence-dare-to-be-bad/) at the things that matter less. I think it applies to people at least as much as to companies. **Choosing what to be bad at is not a failure of ambition. It is what makes being good at anything possible.**

## This Time, Nobody Is Forcing Me

{/* IMAGE PLACEHOLDER: early morning at a home desk, a person seen from behind designing a course: a wall of sticky notes mapping out lessons and modules, a whiteboard with a simple marketing funnel sketch, open books on teaching and learning, a steaming cup of coffee. Sunrise light through the window. No readable text. Calm and deliberate, chosen rather than forced, wide 16:9. */}
<img src="/images/blog/i-was-not-born-for-this/not-forced-this-time.jpg" alt="An early-morning desk covered in course plans, chosen rather than forced" class="w-full h-auto rounded-lg shadow-lg mb-6" />

The list moves in both directions, though, and right now a few items are moving off it.

These days I am teaching myself a number of things that sat on my bad-at list for a long time, among them **how to design a course, how marketing works, and how education itself works**, meaning the craft behind helping people actually learn, not just the content you put in front of them. All of them matter for Skill-Wanderer and for the mission behind it, so they have earned their place in the budget.

It will take time. I know that already. Some of my early attempts will not be good. That is the price, and I have decided to pay it.

But there is one difference this time. **Nobody is forcing me.** Leadership was forced on me by a job. Programming was a leap of faith, with a friend giving me the push. This one is simply a choice. I have looked at what these skills will cost and what they will make possible, and I have decided I do not want to be bad at them anymore.

That, I think, is what it looks like when the old belief is finally gone. Not that you feel born for everything. It is that "can I learn this?" stops being the question, and "is it worth learning right now?" takes its place.

## Cannot, or Chose Not To?

{/* IMAGE PLACEHOLDER: close-up of a hand holding two cards over a wooden table. The left card reads "I CAN'T" and is creased, faded and soft at the edges from years of being carried in a pocket. The right card reads "I CHOSE NOT TO" and is crisp and new. Soft warm light, shallow depth of field, wide 16:9. */}
<img src="/images/blog/i-was-not-born-for-this/two-cards.jpg" alt="Two cards: a worn 'I can't' and a crisp 'I chose not to'" class="w-full h-auto rounded-lg shadow-lg mb-6" />

So here is what I would like you to take away, and it fits in a single question.

**Next time you catch yourself thinking you are bad at something, ask yourself: do you believe you cannot learn it, or did you deliberately choose not to?**

Be honest with the answer, because the two lead to very different places.

**If you chose not to,** good. Own it. Put it on your list next to my badminton and my driving, and do not waste a single minute feeling guilty about it. That is not a weakness. It is a strategy, and everyone who has ever been excellent at something keeps a long list just like it.

**If you believe you cannot,** look hard at where that belief came from. A test you failed once, years ago? A teacher's offhand comment? A film where the hero was simply born brilliant? Then remember what the belief does: it is 100% right only for as long as you keep believing it. You do not have to become certain that you can. You only have to stop being certain that you cannot. That moves you from 100% to 50%, and 50% is a chance.

Then turn it into a choice, whichever way it goes. Give it a small, honest test: a few weeks, one small project, the smallest version of the thing. Not to prove you were born for it, but to find out what you can learn. You might decide it is worth the time and keep going. You might decide it is not, and move it, honestly this time, into the "chose not to" column. Both are fine. **The only answer that is not fine is the one you never actually chose.**

This is why, if I could give my learners only one thing, it would be the ability to self-learn. And self-learning starts one step earlier than most people think. Not with a course, a book, or a plan, but with a quiet decision to stop believing the sentence that has been keeping you out.

You were probably not born for it. Almost nobody was. **You can still learn it, if you decide it is worth your time.**

So tell me: what is on your list? Which of the things you are bad at did you choose, and which ones did you only ever believe?
