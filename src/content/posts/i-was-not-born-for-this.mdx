If there is one thing I want every learner who passes through Skill-Wanderer to walk away with, it is not a framework, a language, or a certificate.

It is **the ability to self-learn.**

Frameworks expire. Languages drift out of fashion. Certificates quietly go stale in a drawer. The ability to pick up the next thing on your own is the one skill I know of that keeps paying out, whatever the industry decides to do next.

In [my last post](/blog/the-most-important-skill-in-tech-is-learning), I tried to pin down what learning actually is. Since then I have kept turning over self-learning in particular, and I realised there is a step that comes before all of it, so early that most of us walk straight past it:

**Before you decide to learn anything, you have to believe that you can learn it.**

That sounds almost too simple to be worth writing down. It took me years to actually do it. This post is about why, and about how my own disbelief finally broke.

It is also about the other half, the one motivational posters tend to leave out. Being able to learn something is not the same as having to learn it. Some of the things I am bad at, I am bad at on purpose.

## "I Was Not Born for This"

{/* IMAGE PLACEHOLDER: a young adult sitting at a desk, staring in defeat at a thick book wrapped in a chain and closed with a heavy padlock. The key hangs on a cord around their own neck, in plain sight but unnoticed. No text. Soft warm light, quiet and slightly melancholic, wide 16:9. */}
<img src="/images/blog/i-was-not-born-for-this/the-padlock.jpg" alt="A book locked shut, with the key hanging unnoticed around the reader's own neck" class="w-full h-auto rounded-lg shadow-lg mb-6" />

Across my career as a mentor and as a manager, there is one sentence I have heard more than any other, and every time I hear it, it sounds strangely nostalgic:

**"I was not born for this."**

It comes in plenty of costumes. *I am not a numbers person. I am just not technical. I am not a natural leader. Some people just get it, and I am not one of them.* Different words, same sentence underneath.

Here is the uncomfortable thing I have come to believe about it: **if you believe you were not born for something, you are 100% right.** As long as you believe it, and only for as long as you believe it.

Think about what the belief does. If you are convinced you were not born to code, you never open the editor. If you open it anyway, the first error message reads like a verdict instead of a clue, and you close it again. If you were not born to lead, you turn down the team lead role, or you take it and spend every meeting waiting to be found out. Either way, you never collect the hundreds of small, clumsy attempts that turn a beginner into someone competent.

So the belief does not describe your ability. **It manufactures the evidence that proves it right.**

The good news is hidden in the last part: *only for as long as you believe it.* The belief needs you to keep feeding it. The moment you stop, it stops being true.

That is easy for me to say now. It was not easy to live, because for a long time I was the one saying it. Which is probably why it sounds so nostalgic.

## Two Things I Was Sure I Was Not Born For

{/* IMAGE PLACEHOLDER: a university student at a cluttered dorm desk at night, leaning back with arms crossed, deliberately pushing two objects to the far edge of the desk: a laptop whose screen is full of red error messages, and a team captain's armband. Through the window, a campus at dusk. No readable text. Nostalgic film-photo look, warm tungsten light, wide 16:9. */}
<img src="/images/blog/i-was-not-born-for-this/the-two-beliefs.jpg" alt="A student pushing away a laptop full of errors and a captain's armband" class="w-full h-auto rounded-lg shadow-lg mb-6" />

From childhood all the way through university, I carried a long list of things I believed I was simply not born for. Two of them matter for this story, mostly because they turned out to be the two things my working life would be built on.

**I believed I was not born to be a leader.** And about the "born" part, I was right. I was not born a leader. That is not modesty. There is no doubt about it whatsoever.

**I believed I was not born for programming.** My reasoning felt airtight. I am not a very careful person, so I knew I would make bugs. And in my head, programmers were careful, precise people who wrote correct code. Very obviously not me.

Underneath both sat a simpler belief: **talent was something you were born with.** You either had it or you did not, and the people who had it were visible from a mile away. Leaders led. Programmers programmed. Everyone else watched.

The tricky part is that none of this ever felt like a belief. It felt like a plain fact about me, as fixed as my height, and it is hard to question something you do not even recognise as a belief.

And the culture around me did an excellent job of confirming it.

## We Only Ever Meet Them at the Top

{/* IMAGE PLACEHOLDER: a dark cinema. On the screen, a heroic figure stands triumphant on a summit in golden light. Behind the audience, the projector's film reel has spilled across the floor, and the spilled frames show everything that was cut: someone practising alone at night, falling, studying, failing, repeating the same motion again and again. No text. Cinematic, high contrast, wide 16:9. */}
<img src="/images/blog/i-was-not-born-for-this/the-cut-footage.jpg" alt="The triumph on screen, and the years of practice left on the cutting room floor" class="w-full h-auto rounded-lg shadow-lg mb-6" />

Think about how we meet great people in films, in books, and in the stories we grow up on.

We meet the general on the morning of the decisive battle, already brilliant. We meet the founder on stage, already successful. We meet the leader in the middle of the famous speech, already magnetic. The years before, the awkward ones, the failed ones, the boring ones, either never make it into the story or get squeezed into a two-minute training montage with good music.

I understand why. Nobody wants to watch ten years of someone slowly becoming slightly less bad at something. But it leaves us with a distorted picture. **We see great people when they are great. We almost never see how they became great.** So we fill the gap with the simplest explanation available: they must have been born that way.

That is exactly the conclusion I drew as a kid, and I held on to it for a long time.

The highlight reel did not stay in the cinema, either. These days it follows us everywhere. Social media shows us the promotion, the launch and the finished project, and almost never the years in between. In tech, a junior developer looks at a senior's clean, confident code and assumes the senior was simply born for this. They never saw the senior's first project. Nobody posts that one.

So we end up comparing our own raw footage with everyone else's final cut. No wonder so many of us decide we were not born for it.

## Most Great Leaders Were Not Born Great

{/* IMAGE PLACEHOLDER: a museum gallery wall with five grand framed portraits of historical leaders, faces softly lost in shadow so no one is identifiable. Beside each grand portrait hangs a small, plain pencil sketch of the same figure before: a nervous young lawyer in a courtroom, a frontier boy reading by firelight in a log cabin, a shop clerk behind a counter of leather goods, a teacher at a chalkboard, a peasant boy holding a pair of straw sandals. No text or plaques. Soft gallery lighting, quiet and reverent, wide 16:9. */}
<img src="/images/blog/i-was-not-born-for-this/before-the-portraits.jpg" alt="Five dark portrait frames, each above a humble sketch of who that leader was before" class="w-full h-auto rounded-lg shadow-lg mb-6" />

To be fair to my younger self, some people really are born with traits that give them a head start in leadership. But a head start is not the whole race. Leadership, like most skills, is mostly built the unglamorous way: practice, feedback, getting things wrong and adjusting.

And once you go looking for the part the films leave out, you find it almost everywhere.

As a young lawyer in Bombay, **Mohandas Gandhi** stood up to cross-examine a witness in his very first case, and could not think of a single question to ask. His heart, he later wrote, sank into his boots. He sat back down and handed back his fee. That same man went on to lead India's independence movement.

**Abraham Lincoln** was born in a log cabin and had, by his own estimate, less than a year of schooling in total. He taught himself law from borrowed books. He lost his first election, then failed twice to get into the US Senate, and only after all of that became president. For a post about self-learning, it is hard to find a better example.

At 38, **Ulysses S. Grant** was a clerk in his father's leather shop. He had left the army years earlier, failed at farming, sold firewood on the streets of St. Louis to get by, and watched one small business after another go nowhere. Less than four years later, he commanded every army of the Union. Nobody looking at that clerk would have picked him out as a born general.

Closer to home, [**Võ Nguyên Giáp**](/blog/chatgpt-called-me-unconventional-it-unlocked-a-lesson-from-vietnams-greatest-general) was a history teacher with no formal military training who taught himself strategy. And [**Toyotomi Hideyoshi**](/blog/the-tanegashima-shift-the-tragedy-and-the-ascension-of-the-samurai-coder), born a peasant without even a surname, started out carrying Oda Nobunaga's sandals and ended up ruling Japan. I have written about both of them before, if you want the longer stories.

None of them were born great. The greatness came later, after years of learning that the highlight reel leaves out.

As for me, I was certainly not born a leader. It turns out that puts me in rather good company.

## Nobody Was Born a Programmer

{/* IMAGE PLACEHOLDER: a single wooden desk holding three generations of programming side by side. On the left, a 1940s engineering logbook with a real moth taped onto the page beside illegible handwritten notes. In the middle, a 1980s continuous-feed code printout covered in red pen corrections. On the right, a modern laptop showing a failing test in red next to an AI chat panel. Warm desk-lamp light, documentary still-life style, wide 16:9. */}
<img src="/images/blog/i-was-not-born-for-this/every-era-has-bugs.jpg" alt="A logbook moth, a 1980s printout full of red ink, and a modern failing test" class="w-full h-auto rounded-lg shadow-lg mb-6" />

The programming belief rested on a different misunderstanding, and it took me an embarrassingly long time to see it.

I thought good programmers did not make bugs. What I did not know back then is that **every developer makes bugs.** All of them, including the very best. These days even AI makes them, which makes sense when you remember who it learned to code from: us.

So the skill was never "do not make bugs". **The skill is catching them**: testing, reviewing, debugging, and building habits that turn your carelessness into something manageable. Every one of those is learned. Nobody is born knowing how to read a stack trace.

And there is a simpler argument still: **nobody was born a programmer.** For the first generation it was literally impossible, because when they were born there was nothing to program yet. They had to work it out for themselves, and everyone since has had to learn it too. Me included.

AI has shifted the picture a little. Today someone who has never studied programming can get a simple app working, and that is a real change. But building something real still takes learning: knowing what to ask for, and knowing when the answer is wrong. (I wrote more about that in [the middle way on AI](/blog/the-middle-way-ai-is-neither-stupid-nor-a-silver-bullet) and [leading AI agents](/blog/leading-ai-agents-is-not-much-different-from-leading-people).) The tools changed *what* you have to learn. They did not remove the need to learn it.

## 100% Right, or 50% Right

{/* IMAGE PLACEHOLDER: an ancient Roman-era boat race on a bright Mediterranean sea. Two long oared ships near a rocky turning marker; the trailing crew rows with fierce determination, spray flying, closing the gap on the leading ship by inches. No text. Painterly, epic and sunlit, in the style of a classical oil painting, wide 16:9. */}
<img src="/images/blog/i-was-not-born-for-this/they-can-because-they-think-they-can.jpg" alt="A trailing crew in an ancient boat race, closing the gap on the leader" class="w-full h-auto rounded-lg shadow-lg mb-6" />

There is a saying I have carried around for years, and I have never managed to shake it:

> **If you go into a battle believing you are going to lose, you are 100% right. If you go in believing you are going to win, you are right maybe 50% of the time.**

I like it because it is more honest than most motivational quotes. It does not promise that believing in yourself makes you win. It only points out the asymmetry. **Disbelief guarantees the loss. Belief merely keeps the outcome open.** With 50%, you have a chance. With a guaranteed loss, you have none.

The idea is old. Two thousand years ago, Virgil wrote about a boat race in which the crew chasing the leader is summed up in one line: "they can, because they think they can." What gets quoted far less often is how the race ends. They drew almost level, and still finished second. Virgil understood the 50% too.

In learning, the battle is rarely dramatic. It is the evening you decide whether to open the tutorial or put it off again. It is the moment after your third failed attempt, when you either try a fourth time or quietly decide you are just not a technical person. Nobody sees those moments. But that is exactly where the 100% does its work.

**Believing you can learn something does not make you learn it.** It does not replace the practice, the good teacher, or the hours. It only makes them possible. Belief does not win the battle. It gets you onto the field.

For most of my early life, I went into the battle believing I would lose. And I was 100% right.

## How the Belief Finally Broke

{/* IMAGE PLACEHOLDER: dusk at a busy container port. A lone figure in office clothes, holding a folder of shipping documents, stands at the edge of the quay. In front of them, a narrow footbridge made of softly glowing lines of code stretches out over the water toward a distant city of lit screens. The bridge is still being built, one plank appearing at a time just ahead of their feet. Cranes and stacked containers behind them. No readable text. Cinematic and hopeful, wide 16:9. */}
<img src="/images/blog/i-was-not-born-for-this/the-leap.jpg" alt="From a container port to a bridge of code, built one plank at a time" class="w-full h-auto rounded-lg shadow-lg mb-6" />

It only started to change when two things happened.

{/* TODO: confirm the book. The draft said "a Mark Anthony book", and I could not pin down which author or title was meant. Put the exact title here, and if one idea from it cracked the belief, quote it in this paragraph. */}

The first was a book by Mark Anthony. A book cannot make you a leader, but the right one can make you suspicious of the story you have been telling yourself, and that is what this one did.

The second was not something I chose at all. Through a chain of events I still cannot decide was unfortunate or fortunate, I became an import and export manager. The job was about finding and reaching customers overseas, and it came with the one thing I had spent my whole life believing I was not built for: people to lead.

There was no opting out. The role needed a leader, and I was the one in it. Whether I felt born for it was beside the point.

It was never easy. But somewhere along the way, I noticed two things. I was improving. And **I was not half as bad as I had thought.** A good part of the distance between the leader I feared I was and the leader I actually was had been made of belief.

What changed me was not inspiration, and not confidence. The book planted the doubt, but the real work was done by practising every day, with real stakes and real feedback.

The programming belief went the same way, except this time I jumped instead of being pushed.

While I was still in import and export, our company set out to build its first website, and out of pure curiosity I took an HTML and CSS course on a free [LinkedIn Learning](/blog/the-skill-wanderers-playbook-a-deep-dive-review-of-linkedin-learning) trial. That was the spark. A friend, who became one of my early mentors, eventually convinced me to quit my import and export job and move into tech properly. It was a leap of faith. And yes, there were bugs, plenty of them, exactly as I had predicted all those years earlier. But by then I knew what I had not known as a student: everybody makes them. The bugs were never proof that I was not born for this. They were simply part of the job.

Neither move proved that I had secretly been born for it all along. I had not been. What they proved was the thing I had stubbornly refused to believe: **you do not need to be born with it, because you can learn it.**

## Anything, but Not Everything

{/* IMAGE PLACEHOLDER: a small market stall where skills are sold as objects: a stethoscope, a badminton racket, a car key, a laptop, a microphone, a paintbrush, a stack of course notes. Instead of prices, each item carries a paper tag with a small hourglass drawn on it, the sand at a different level on each. A customer's hand hovers over them, choosing. No text. Warm afternoon light, thoughtful mood, wide 16:9. */}
<img src="/images/blog/i-was-not-born-for-this/paying-in-time.jpg" alt="A market stall of skills, each priced in time rather than money" class="w-full h-auto rounded-lg shadow-lg mb-6" />

Here is where I want to be careful, because this is the point where posts like this usually tip over into motivational-poster territory.

"You can learn anything!" True, as far as it goes. But **being able to learn anything does not mean you can learn everything. And it certainly does not mean you should.**

Every skill has a price. Some of it is **money**: courses, books, equipment. Most of it is **time**: the hours of practice, and the months of being bad at something before you are merely okay at it. And some doors are not locked by ability at all. I genuinely believe you could teach yourself medicine from textbooks and online lectures, but that would not make you a doctor. Some doors are locked by licences and years of supervised training, and for very good reasons.

So I think about learning a bit like shopping. You can afford almost anything, but not everything at once, and every purchase rules out a few others. **The currency that matters most here is time, because it is the one you can never earn back.** If you live to eighty, you get roughly four thousand weeks, as Oliver Burkeman points out in his book of the same name. Every skill you take on is paid for out of that budget, and every yes to one skill is a quiet no to another, whether you notice it or not.

Being mindful of that is not pessimism. It is simply taking your own time seriously.

## The Things I Choose to Be Bad At

{/* IMAGE PLACEHOLDER: a bright, tidy room. On a shelf, a badminton racket and a car key sit under a light layer of dust, clearly untouched for a long time. Below them, a desk in active use: a well-worn laptop, open notebooks, sticky notes. Through the window, a taxi waits outside. No text. Calm, content, gently humorous, wide 16:9. */}
<img src="/images/blog/i-was-not-born-for-this/chosen-to-be-bad.jpg" alt="A dusty badminton racket and car key, with a well-used desk and a taxi waiting outside" class="w-full h-auto rounded-lg shadow-lg mb-6" />

Which brings me to a list I am oddly proud of: the things I am bad at on purpose.

**I am a bad badminton player.** Not because I cannot learn badminton. I am quite sure I could, given the hours. I am bad at it because I chose to be. When I weigh what getting good would cost me in time against what it would give back, I see very little benefit for the life I actually live. So I leave it alone, and I am entirely at peace with it.

**I cannot drive a car.** Again, not because I am incapable of learning. I can take a taxi anywhere I need or want to go, and I do not travel by car much anyway. Learning to drive, and keeping the skill sharp, would cost real time for very little return.

The list goes on. But almost every item on it got there **by decision**, not by destiny.

From the outside, "cannot" and "chose not to" look identical. Either way, I will not be beating you at badminton or driving you to the airport. From the inside, they could not be more different. One is a belief about my limits. The other is a decision about my priorities. One closes a door I never actually tried. The other walks past a door I know I could open, because I would rather spend the time behind a different one.

**Choosing what to be bad at is not a failure of ambition. It is what makes being good at anything possible.**

## This Time, Nobody Is Forcing Me

{/* IMAGE PLACEHOLDER: early morning at a home desk, a person seen from behind designing a course: a wall of sticky notes mapping out lessons and modules, a whiteboard with a simple marketing funnel sketch, open books on teaching and learning, a steaming cup of coffee. Sunrise light through the window. No readable text. Calm and deliberate, chosen rather than forced, wide 16:9. */}
<img src="/images/blog/i-was-not-born-for-this/not-forced-this-time.jpg" alt="An early-morning desk covered in course plans, chosen rather than forced" class="w-full h-auto rounded-lg shadow-lg mb-6" />

The list moves in both directions, though, and right now a few items are moving off it.

These days I am teaching myself things that sat on my bad-at list for a long time: **how to design a course, how marketing works, and how education itself works**, meaning the craft of helping people actually learn, not just the content you put in front of them. All of them matter for Skill-Wanderer and the mission behind it, so they have earned their place in the budget.

Some of that learning is already visible on this blog. A year ago I wrote that [I never once thought I would launch a course](/blog/victory-belongs-to-the-most-persevering-our-first-course-is-live), and then launched one anyway. Since then the curriculum has [gone AI-first](/blog/wanderers-log-3-the-tet-reflection-why-skill-wanderer-is-going-ai-first), and we [decided to leave Moodle and build our own learning platform](/blog/wanderers-log-4-goodbye-moodle-hello-custom-lms). None of it would have happened if I still believed I was not born for this.

There is still a long way to go. Some of my early attempts will not be good. That is the price, and I have decided to pay it.

But there is one difference this time. **Nobody is forcing me.** Leadership was forced on me by a job. Programming was a leap of faith, with a friend giving me the push. This one is simply a choice.

And choosing changes how the bad early attempts feel. When you have agreed to the price in advance, a clumsy first try is not proof that you were not born for this. It is just part of the payment.

That, I think, is what it looks like when the old belief is finally gone. You do not suddenly feel born for everything. "Can I learn this?" just stops being the question, and "is it worth learning right now?" takes its place.

## Cannot, or Chose Not To?

{/* IMAGE PLACEHOLDER: close-up of a hand holding two cards over a wooden table. The left card reads "I CAN'T" and is creased, faded and soft at the edges from years of being carried in a pocket. The right card reads "I CHOSE NOT TO" and is crisp and new. Soft warm light, shallow depth of field, wide 16:9. */}
<img src="/images/blog/i-was-not-born-for-this/two-cards.jpg" alt="Two cards: a worn 'I can't' and a crisp 'I chose not to'" class="w-full h-auto rounded-lg shadow-lg mb-6" />

So here is what I would like you to take away, and it fits in a single question.

**Next time you catch yourself thinking you are bad at something, ask yourself: do you believe you cannot learn it, or did you choose not to?**

**If you chose not to,** good. Own it. Put it on your list next to my badminton and my driving, and do not waste a single minute feeling guilty about it. That is not a weakness. It is a strategy.

**If you believe you cannot,** look hard at where that belief came from. A test you failed once, years ago? A teacher's offhand comment? A film where the hero was simply born brilliant? Then remember: the belief is 100% right only for as long as you keep believing it. You do not have to become certain that you can. You only have to stop being certain that you cannot. That moves you from 100% to 50%, and 50% is a chance.

Then make it a real choice, whichever way it goes. Give it a small, honest test: a few weeks, one small project. You might decide it is worth your time and keep going. You might decide it is not, and move it, honestly this time, into the "chose not to" column. Both are fine. **The only answer that is not fine is the one you never actually chose.**

This is why, if I could give my learners only one thing, it would be the ability to self-learn. And self-learning starts one step earlier than most people think: not with a course or a plan, but with a quiet decision to stop believing the sentence that has been keeping you out.

You were probably not born for it. Almost nobody was. **You can still learn it, if you decide it is worth your time.**

So tell me: what is on your list? Which of the things you are bad at did you choose, and which ones did you only ever believe?
